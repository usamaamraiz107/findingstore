Product Sourcing Guide: How to Find Reliable Suppliers

Whether you run a small online shop, sell on a marketplace, or just want to buy something in bulk for less, sooner or later you will need to find a supplier. Product sourcing is the process of finding who actually makes or wholesales a product, checking that they are reliable, and buying from them at a price that makes sense. This guide covers the main sourcing options, how to evaluate suppliers, and the mistakes beginners most often make.

Start with a clear product definition

Before contacting anyone, define the product precisely: materials, dimensions, colours, packaging, quantities and the quality level you need. "Phone case" is not a specification. "Shock-resistant TPU case for a specific phone model, matte black, in a retail box with your logo, 500 units" is. A precise definition lets you compare quotes fairly and protects you if the delivered goods do not match.

If you only have a picture of the product, a visual search tool can help you find its common names and the words sellers use for it. Upload the photo to FindingStore or another reverse image search, then note the recurring keywords in the result titles. Those keywords are what you will use to search supplier platforms.

The main sourcing channels

Wholesale marketplaces. Platforms such as Alibaba, 1688 (the Chinese domestic version, usually cheaper but in Chinese), Made-in-China and Global Sources connect buyers with manufacturers and trading companies. They are good for larger orders and custom branding, but require you to negotiate and to check suppliers carefully.

Retail marketplaces with small quantities. AliExpress, Temu and similar sites let you buy single units or small batches at prices close to wholesale. They are useful for testing products and samples, though prices per unit are higher than true wholesale.

Dropshipping and fulfilment agents. Services such as CJdropshipping and other sourcing agents find products for you, hold stock, and ship each order directly to your customer. You pay a little more per item but avoid holding inventory.

Local wholesalers and markets. Wholesale markets in your own city can beat imports once you include shipping and customs, and you can inspect goods in person. Many local wholesalers also sell through social media and messaging apps.

Trade shows. Events such as the Canton Fair, or regional industry exhibitions, let you meet suppliers face to face and see samples. They require travel, but the relationships can be valuable.

Manufacturer or trading company?

Many sellers on wholesale platforms are trading companies, not factories. That is not necessarily bad. Trading companies often speak better English, accept smaller orders, and handle several product types. Factories may offer lower prices and more control over customisation, but usually want larger orders. Ask directly, look for factory photos and certifications, and check the business licence information on the platform.

How to evaluate a supplier

  • Time in business and verification. Prefer suppliers with several years on the platform and third-party verification badges.
  • Transaction history and reviews. Look at the volume of past orders and read negative reviews for recurring problems such as late delivery or wrong sizes.
  • Response quality. Clear, specific answers to technical questions are a good sign. Vague replies and pressure to pay quickly are not.
  • Samples. Always order samples before a bulk order, ideally from two or three suppliers so you can compare.
  • Payment protection. Pay through the platform's trade assurance or escrow system rather than direct bank transfers to personal accounts.

Understanding the real cost

The unit price on a supplier page is just the start. Your landed cost per unit includes:

  1. Unit price at your order quantity (prices fall as quantity rises).
  2. Packaging, labelling or branding charges.
  3. Shipping from the supplier to you (air is fast and expensive, sea is slow and cheap for large volumes).
  4. Import duty, sales tax and customs clearance fees in your country.
  5. Payment fees and currency conversion.
  6. An allowance for defective units, typically a few percent.

Add these up before you decide whether a product is profitable or whether buying in bulk really saves money compared with buying locally.

Negotiating sensibly

Suppliers expect some negotiation, but the best results come from being a credible, clear buyer rather than from aggressive haggling. Share your target quantity and timeline, ask for tiered pricing, and ask what would lower the cost, such as simpler packaging or a standard colour. Be wary of a quote far below everyone else's: it often means lower-quality materials or a supplier who will raise the price after you commit.

Quality control

For larger orders, agree the specification in writing, including tolerances and packaging, and consider a pre-shipment inspection by an independent inspection company. For smaller orders, ask the supplier for photos and videos of your actual goods before they ship. Keep all communication on the platform so you have a record if you need to open a dispute.

Legal and safety checks

Some products need certifications or approvals to be sold legally, such as electrical safety marks, food-contact approvals, toy safety standards or telecoms registration. Branded goods offered at very low prices are often counterfeit, and selling them can get your shop banned or lead to legal trouble. Check your country's import rules before ordering anything with batteries, liquids, cosmetics, electronics or medical claims.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Ordering in bulk without samples.
  • Comparing unit prices without shipping and duties.
  • Relying on a single supplier with no backup.
  • Paying outside the platform to get a small discount.
  • Choosing products that are fragile, oversized or seasonal without accounting for the extra risk.

Summary

Good sourcing is mostly careful checking: a precise product definition, several supplier quotes, samples, a full landed-cost calculation and protected payments. Use visual search to identify products and their market prices, compare those prices with supplier quotes, and you will quickly see which products are worth pursuing.
